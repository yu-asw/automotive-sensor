First, why two students is an advantage

This course was originally designed for a class of twenty. Had it run that way, four of you would have shared a single board, and you would have spent most of the class watching someone else do the work. With two students, this becomes possible instead.

One condition comes attached. With two of you there is nowhere to hide. Every week you have to record what you did and explain it, and if one of you stops, the whole project stops. This plan is written on that assumption.

01

What this course covers

Self-driving and ADAS both start at the sensors that stand in for the human eye. This course covers the six environment-perception sensors on a vehicle: how they work, how to read their specifications, and how to interface with them and get their data out.

Camera Ultrasonic Radar Lidar GNSS IMU

The boundary is deliberate. This course goes as far as "what physical principle a sensor uses to measure the world, and in what form that data arrives in software." The algorithms that process that data belong to the courses that follow.

TopicCourse
Sensor principles · specifications · interfaces · data acquisitionAutomotive Sensor (this course, year 3 fall)
Learning algorithms — CNN, RNN and the restDeep Learning (year 3 fall, recommended alongside)
2D image and 3D point-cloud signal processing, object recognitionAutomotive Vision (year 4 spring)
Advanced sensor fusion, navigation · perception · controlAutonomous Driving (year 4 fall)

OUTCOMEWhat you will be able to do afterwards

  1. Explain how each of the six sensors works down to the physics, and read the numbers that matter on a datasheet (resolution · FOV · range resolution · detection range).
  2. Wire a sensor up yourself and get data out of it on an embedded board, over GPIO · I2C · UART · MIPI-CSI/V4L2 · GStreamer.
  3. Calibrate a camera, and explain the intrinsic and extrinsic parameters and the distortion model.
  4. Read camera · lidar · radar data from public real-vehicle datasets (KITTI, nuScenes), visualize it and do a first analysis.
  5. Compare what each sensor is good and bad at (light · weather · range · resolution) and explain why sensor fusion is necessary.
  6. Integrate several sensors into a single real-time system, and make that system work on a moving vehicle. ← added because there are only two of you
02

The semester project — a sensor-integrated self-driving mini car

PLATFORMPlatform

The two of you build one 1/10-scale self-driving vehicle together, on a TOPST D3-G (Telechips TCC8050, a Korean automotive SoC) with a PiRacer chassis. The software is Ubuntu 22.04 + ROS2 Humble. Every sensor gets its own ROS2 node and the nodes hand data to each other over topics — the same structure a real autonomous-driving software stack uses.

This platform (D-Racer) is the official platform of the 2026 SEA:ME hackathon (sponsored by Volkswagen Group Korea, hosted by Kookmin University). What you build this semester can go straight into the competition, or into capstone design.

The order is "get the car driving first, then put the sensors on one at a time." Two weeks of groundwork → drive the car with the camera (weeks 3–6) → add the lidar (weeks 7–9) → add the radar (weeks 10–12) → integrate (weeks 13–15). Every time a sensor goes on, the same four steps repeat: principle → wiring and acquisition → turn it into a vehicle capability → demo and record.

DEMOFinal demo scenario · week 15

On an indoor track, the vehicle does the following.

1CameraLane-following autonomous driving — one full lap of the track
2Ultrasonic LidarDetect an obstacle ahead, then stop or steer around it — AEB in miniature
3RadarMeasure the speed of an approaching object from the Doppler shift
4GNSS IMULog the driven trajectory and visualize it afterwards
5All sixAll six streams merged live onto ROS2 topics and shown on the dashboard

ROLESWho owns what

You share one platform, and on it each of you has an assigned subsystem. The assignment is what you are responsible for delivering — it is not the scope of what you learn. The lectures and the midterm cover all six sensors, and both of you study all six.

Shared by both: system architecture, the vehicle control node (motor and servo), integration, the dashboard, and the final demo.

Peer teaching is a graded item. At every lab meeting you brief the other person on your own progress, and just before week 8 and again in week 14 you sit an oral check on the other person's sensors. There are only two of you, so each of you is the other's only audience and only reviewer.

MILESTONESFour milestones

M1END WK 4

Platform alive

D3-G boots, ROS2 nodes talk to each other, motor and servo under remote control drive 3 m in a straight line, CSI camera streams video

M2END WK 6

Camera autonomy

One lap of the track (straights and gentle curves) driving on detected lane lines; stops when the ultrasonic sees something within 30 cm ahead

M3END WK 9

Lidar integrated

2D lidar gives range and bearing to an obstacle ahead, and the car slows down or steers around it rather than stopping; no conflict with the camera-based driving

M4END WK 12

Radar integrated

Doppler speed measurements logged, approach warning working, camera · lidar · radar topics published simultaneously

The final demo (week 15) is judged on all of that plus the ultrasonic and IMU: all six sensors publishing at once, a driving log (rosbag), and the dashboard.

Milestones are not scored pass/fail. They are scored on the attempt and on the record of it. A failed attempt can still earn full marks if the lab notebook holds an analysis of the cause and the next hypothesis.

Half of any hardware project fails — that is the normal state. Do not hide it, write it down.

How the course runs

SESSIONThe three-hour session

0:00 – 1:3090-minute lecture10-minute lab meeting (each of you briefs last week's progress, where you are stuck, this week's plan) + the principle, specifications and interface of the week's sensor (both of you together)
1:30 – 3:0090-minute labConnect the week's sensor → acquire data → fold it into the vehicle. I am in the room; when you get stuck, we dig it out on the spot

LOGBOOKWeekly lab notebook · 20% of the grade

Commit it as Markdown to your GitHub repository every week. It is graded on care and specificity, not length.

  1. 01Theory summaryThree to five lines on what you learned this week. Write down what you did not understand, as it is
  2. 02Progress logWhat you did / where you got stuck / what you tried / next week's plan
  3. 03EvidenceMeasurements, plots, screenshots, oscilloscope captures, failure logs
  4. 04Source codeCommitted to the same repository

By the end of the semester that repository is your portfolio. In a job interview, a graduate application or a hackathon, what proves what you built is this record, not a transcript.

DATASET LABDataset labs

The project vehicle uses low-cost sensors. For real data from vehicle-grade sensors (3D lidar, 77 GHz radar) you work with public real-vehicle datasets instead: KITTI lidar point clouds in week 8, the nuScenes radar lab in week 11, both run on your own laptop (Colab is fine).

05

Grading

PROJECT 50LOGBOOK 20MIDTERM 2010
Project 50% — four milestones 20% + final demo 20% + final report 10% Weekly lab notebook 20% — 14 submissions; each one missed costs marks Midterm concept check 20% — 30-minute written paper at the start of the week 8 lecture, covering weeks 1–7 Participation and peer teaching 10% — lab-meeting briefings, two oral checks

There is no final exam; the final demo, presentation and report take its place.

The final report is submitted individually and contains: (1) the architecture of the whole system, (2) the sensor principles · specifications · implementation · measurements for your own subsystem, (3) what failed and why, (4) your own conclusion on the limits of each sensor and why sensor fusion is needed, (5) what you would improve.

If you want both, that is also a good answer — in the back half of the semester you will end up in each other's code anyway.
